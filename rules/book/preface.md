# Preface

I wrote *Sinew & Steel* out of a familiar frustration with role-playing games I otherwise loved: the adventure in my head often felt lighter than the system built to carry it.

Some games buried the table in rules. Others were elegant until play put pressure on them: one GM’s “that seems fair” became another’s “the system doesn’t allow that”, and exceptions piled up. Even old-school play, sharp and memorable as it was, often carried janky logic: awkward saves, punishing randomness, strange gaps, and rules that felt like historical artefacts more than a coherent game engine.

My first RPG experiences came in gamebooks, solo adventures, and boxed sets that got you moving quickly. You made decisions, took risks, bled for them, and the fictional worlds remembered what happened. I wanted that again: a game *fast to start and infinite to extend*, with the old illogic and clutter pared away.

So I built one, with a lean chassis, intuitive and consistent mechanics, visible odds, and enough structure to let the table improvise without the game wobbling.

This is simply the way I like RPGs most.

## What this game is trying to do

Sinew & Steel (S&S) makes a few promises:

- One roll resolves the decisive moment. Talk comes first, the stakes are clear before anyone rolls, and the dice come out only when the outcome is uncertain and it matters.
- Every point counts, and the odds stay transparent. Raise a stat by one and your chance of success goes up by one in twenty. Spend a resource now and you know what you give up later.
- Consequences are visible and tracked. The game gives you a tension fuse you can watch climb until it finally blows.
- Characters are yours to build, and every strength has a price.
- One chassis carries many genres. Each gets its own skin, but the rules underneath stay the same.

## A narrower taste

S&S follows a particular set of tastes:

- Fictional positioning carries the weight that crunchy games give to stacked situational modifiers.
- One engine handles everything. Skins add a few procedures, written in the core’s terms.
- Skins set the frame, and your table fills in the world. There is no complete setting bible to learn first.

Games that go the other way produce great campaigns for plenty of tables. S&S is for people who want a game they can hold in their heads and run with little prep.

## Why these choices?

Four design decisions run through the whole book.

*Roll-under d20 with margin* makes ability feel direct. Your stat is the target number, so your odds are written on your sheet. How far under it you roll is your margin, and the game puts it to work. Margin settles opposed tests, adds damage in a fight, and shows how well you succeeded or how badly you missed.

*Advantage and Disadvantage* let the fiction move the odds without a pile of modifiers. Roll two dice and keep the better or the worse. The rule is easy to remember and hard to abuse, and it works the same way in opposed tests as in simple checks.

*Luck as score and spendable pool* forces a trade-off between the present and the future. Spend Luck to twist a moment in your favour, and your next test of pure fortune gets harder, because you roll under what you have left. That fits the fiction too. Fortune can still smile on you, but every favour you ask of it leaves less in reserve.

*Pressure as a short fuse* puts a number on something most tables leave untracked: the strain that builds as you take risks. You can push, gamble, and keep going until Pressure reaches 5 and triggers a crisis. Then the fuse resets and the climb begins again. The game remembers the strain so the Custodian (S&S’s name for the GM) doesn’t have to.

## Why skins?

Skins are my compromise between one system that sings in many genres and ten separate rulebooks. A skin is a small overlay that renames the stats, gives Pressure a name and crises that suit the genre, and sets out which consequences fit. The engine does not change.

If you’ve played widely, you’ll recognise the traditions several skins draw on: dungeon-crawl austerity, fogbound horror, blue-book space commerce, wanderer-fantasy elegy, and so on. I keep the nods subtle on purpose. Each skin tries to capture how its genre plays at the table, and every one runs on the same dice.

And if you want to build your own, the Customisation chapter will help you keep the original engine intact.

## How to use this book

If you want to play tonight:

1. Read the Quickstart.
2. Read the Clanfire skin (it’s the closest to “pure core”).
3. Run the Emberfall scenario.

That’s enough. Truly!

If you want to go deeper:

- Players: read The Adventurer preamble, then the Adventurer’s Manual.
- Custodians: read The Custodian preamble, then the Custodian’s Almanac.
- When you want another genre, pick the skin that suits your mood. The skins are arranged in order of complexity, so you can also work through them in sequence.

## A note on AI Custodians

Sinew & Steel is designed so that an AI can run it consistently, which makes it unusually friendly to solo play and to groups without a human GM. The complete tabletop game stands on its own. The AI for Solo Play chapters at the back of the book cover the practical side: keeping secrets, avoiding needless rolls, and making sure player choices change the story. The project’s online repository, <https://github.com/BWBrook/sinew-and-steel>, also holds a free harness that lets an AI agent run S&S campaigns, with character sheets, trackers and campaign memory kept in files.

## An invitation

Everything here is cut lean on purpose, because bloat is dead weight. The parts depend on each other, so a careless change can knock over more than you intended, but the game is meant to be hacked. House-rule freely, combine skins, and write your own. Just keep an eye on what you trade away each time you add weight.

If this book gives you a system that gets out of the way until it matters, and then matters in a way you can feel, it has done its job.
